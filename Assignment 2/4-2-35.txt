35.	If m and n are any positive integers and mn is a
	perfect square, then m and n are perfect squares.


	First, let's assess whether we think this is true
	or false. I'm going to take a leap of faith here,
	and say that my gut tells me we can prove this is
	false using a counterexample.

	Let's look at the given statement:

	"If m and n are any positive integers, and mn is
	a perfect square, then m and n are perfect squares."

	And negate it:

	"There is at least one case where m and n are positive
	integers, such that mn is a perfect square, and m is not
	a perfect square or n is not a perfect square."

	And then define what a perfect square is:

		"n is a perfect square <=> n = k**2 = k * k
		for some integer k"

	Suppose now that we are looking for values of m and n, 
	where m and n are both positive integers.

	We must prove prove that m or n is not a perfect square,
	despite mn being a perfect square, in order for the
	counterexample to be true.

	Let m, n in Z+, and let k in Z

		m = 2
		n = 8

		mn = 16

		16 is a perfect square, since 4**2 = 4 * 4

		However, neither 2 nor 8 are perfect squares,
		since:

			1 * 1 = 1
			2 * 2 = 4

		There is no integer with k**2 = 2

			2 * 2 = 4
			3 * 3 = 9

		There is no integer with k**2 = 8

	Therefore, there is at least one case where m and n
	are positive integers, such that mn is a perfect square,
	and m is not a perfect square or n is not a perfect square.


Solution:	The statement is false.
