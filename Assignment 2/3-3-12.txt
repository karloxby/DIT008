12.	Let D = E = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}. Write negations
	for each of the following statements and determine
	which is true, the given statement or its negation.

	a.	(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x + y = 1
	b.	(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, x + y = -y
	c.	(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. xy ≥ y
	d.	(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, x ≤ y


	a.	(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x + y = 1

		In English:

		"For all x in D, there is at least one
		y in E, such that x + y is 1."

		Here, we have two different quantifiers,
		for which we have to read from left to right
		when negating.

		Let's break the statement into multiple
		components as we negate:
		
		~((A)x in D,)) is equivalent to:
			(E)x in D s.t.

		~((E)y in E s.t. x + y = 1) is equivalent to
			(A)y in E, x + y ≠ 1

		And then we can put them together into a
		sequence of negated quantifiers. Do also note
		the negated predicate.

		(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, x + y ≠ 1

		In English:

		"There is at least one x in D, such that for
		all y in E, x + y is not 1."

		Since both D and E are finite subsets, we could
		exhaust our options to check whether the given
		statement, or its negation, is true.

		D = E = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}.

		Starting with the given statement:

		(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x + y = 1

		But since the given statement claims that
		x + y = 1 for all x in D, let's first look
		at x = -2.

		-2 + y = 1
		y = 3

		And since 3 does not exist in the subsets,
		we can conclude that the statement is false.

		Which means by definition, that the negated
		statement must be true. We are looking for
		one value of x, where all sums with y are not
		equal to 1. Let's exhaust our options, one x
		value at a time:

		D = E = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}.

		(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, x + y ≠ 1
		

		 x	 y	x + y
		 --------------------
		-2	-2	-4
		-2	-1	-3
		-2	 0	-2
		-2	 1	-1
		-2	 2	 0

		
		The negation of the given statement is
		true.


Solution:	The given statement is false, and its negation
		is true.

		
	b.	(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, x + y = -y

		In English:

		"There is at least one x in D, such that for
		all y in E, x + y is -y."

		We have another statement with two quantifiers,
		so let's follow the same practice.

		~((E)x in D s.t.) is equivalent to:
			(A)x in D,

		~((A)y in E, x + y = -y)) is equivalent to:
			(E)y in E s.t. x + y ≠ -y

		And if we put them together:

		(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x + y ≠ -y

		In English:

		"For all x in D, there is at least one y
		in E, such that x + y is not -y."

		Since the negation of the given statement
		should be valid for all values of x, it is
		appropriate to see whether we can find a case
		where this is not true:

		D = E = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}.

		(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x + y ≠ -y


		 x	 y	x + y
		 --------------------
		-2	-2	-4
		-1	-2	-3
		 0	-1	-1
		 1	-2	-1
		 2	-2	 0


		For all values of x, there is at least one value
		of y such that the sum of x and y is not -y.

		Using exhaustion, and the table above, we've 
		proven that the negation of the given statement
		is true. Therefore, the given statement is false.


Solution:	The given statement is false, and its negation
		is true.


	c.	(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. xy ≥ y
		
		In English:

		"For all x in D, there is at least one y
		in E, such that xy is larger than or equal
		to y."

		Let's negate the statement:

		~((A)x in D,) is equivalent to:
			(E)x in D s.t.

		~((E)y in E s.t. xy ≥ y) is equivalent to:
			(A)y in E, xy < y

		And if we put it together:

		(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, xy < y

		In English:

		"There is at least one x in D, such that
		for all y in E, xy is less than y."

		Again, it feels more appropriate to test whether
		the given statement is true or false:

		D = E = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}.

		(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. xy ≥ y


		 x	 y	xy
		 --------------------
		-2	-2	 4
		-1	-2	 2
		 0	 0	 0
		 1	 2	 2
		 2	 2	 4 

		
		Since there is at least one value of y, where
		xy is larger than or equal to y, for all values
		of x in the subsets, we can conclude that
		the given statement is true. Therefore, the
		negation of the given statement is false.


Solution:	The given statement is true, and its negation
		is false.

		
	d.	(E)x in D s.t. (A)y in E, x ≤ y

		In English:

		"There is at least one x in D, such that
		for all y in E, x is less than or equal to
		y."

		Let's start by negating once more:

		~((E)x in D s.t.) is equivalent to:
			(A)x in D,

		~((A)y in E, x ≤ y) is equivalent to:
			(E)y in E s.t. x > y

		And if we put it together:

		(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x > y

		In English:

		"For all x in D, there is at least one
		y in E, such that x is larger than y."

		Let's check whether the negation is true
		or false first:

		D = E = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2}.

		(A)x in D, (E)y in E s.t. x > y


		 x	 y	x > y
		 --------------------
		-2	-2	False
		

		There is no value for y in the subset, for
		which x is larger than y. Since the given
		statement is true, we can therefore conclude
		that the negation must be false.


Solution:	The given statement is true, and its negation
		is false.
