5.6.18.		Tower of Hanoi with Adjacency Requirement:
		Suppose the same situation as in exercise 17. Let

			bₙ = [
				the minimum number of moves 
				needed to transfer a tower of n
				disks from pole A to Pole B
			]

		a.	Find b₁, b₂, and b₃.
		b.	Find b₄.
		c.	Show that bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁ for each
			integer k ≥ 2, where a₁, a₂, a₃, ... is the
			sequence defined in exercise 17.
		d.	Show that bₖ ≤ 3bₖ₋₁ + 1 for each integer
			k ≥ 2.


		Context regarding Tower of Hanoi:

		Suppose that in addition to the requirement that
		they never move a larger disk on top of a smaller
		one, the priests who move the disks of the Tower
		of Hanoi are also allowed only to move disks one
		by one from one pole to an adjacent pole. Assume
		poles A and C are at the two ends of the row and
		pole B is in the middle.


			|		|		|
			|		|		|
			|		|		|
			|		|		|
		________________________________________________
			A		B		C


		a.	Find b₁, b₂, and b₃.

		Suppose bₙ is the minimum number of moves needed to
		transfer a tower of n disks from Pole A to B.

		We must find b₁, b₂, and b₃.

		Hence, b₁ is the number of moves, and there is only
		the one disk.

		In order to move from A to B, we only need the one
		move and we are done.

		Therefore, b₁ = 1

		Next, we must find b₂, which's two disks.

		We can illustrate this as a series of moves:

			(1) 	Since we are restricted from free
				movement between poles, and only
				able to move the disks to an adjacent
				pole, our only move for the top disk
				is from A to B.

			(2)	We are also restricted from placing
				a larger disk on top of a smaller disk.
				Hence, our next move must be moving the
				top disk from B to C.

			(3)	Now we can move the larger, bottom disk
				from A to B.

			(4)	Which allows us to move the top disk
				on top of the bottom disk from C to B.

		The running total is four steps. Therefore b₂ = 4.

		Now we must find b₃, which are three disks: The
		top disk, the middle disk and the bottom disk.

			Top disk < Middle disk < Bottom disk

		Again, we can illustrate this as a series of moves:

			(1)	We move top disk from A to B.

			(2)	Then the top disk from B to C.

			(3)	Now we can move the middle disk
				from A to B.

			(4)	But before we can move the bottom
				disk, we must move the top disk
				back from C to B.

			(5)	Then the top disk from B to A.

			(6)	Now the middle disk can move
				from B to C.

			(7)	Which allows us to move the top
				disk from A to B

			(8)	And then the top disk from B to C.

			(9)	This opens up the bottom disk to
				move from A to B.

			(10)	Which means in order for the middle
				disk to move to B, we must shuffle the
				top disk from C to B.

			(11)	And again, the top disk from B to A.

			(12)	Now the middle disk can be moved from
				C to B.

			(13)	And our final move is the top disk from
				A to B.

		Therefore, b₃ = 13.

Solution:	b₁ = 1, b₂ = 4 and b₃ = 13


		b.	Find b₄.

		We could use the same manual method for finding b₄. But
		before we do that, let's look at b₁, b₂ and b₃ to see if
		we can find a pattern.

		b₁ is just the one move. It does not tell us much.

		However, b₂ is divided into sequences of moves:

			- Moves 1 through 2: 2 moves
			- Move 3: 1 move
			- Move 4: 1 move

			b₂ = 2 + 1 + 1 = 4

		We won't be able to deduct much from b₂ alone, without
		looking at b₃:

			- Moves 1 through 8: 8 moves
			- Move 9: 1 move
			- Move 10 through 13: 4 moves
			
			b₃ = 8 + 1 + 4
		
		The pattern here is not numerical, but in what each
		sequence of moves accomplishes.

		We know that b₄ involves moving four disks. And in
		order for the largest, bottom disk to be able to move
		once from A to B, we have to transfer all other disks
		to pole C first.

		By following the same exercise as for finding b₁, b₂
		and b₃:

			- Moves 1 through 26: 26 moves
			- Move 27: 1 move
			- Moves 28 through 40: 13 moves

			b₄ = 26 + 1 + 13 = 40

		Therefore b₄ = 40.

Solution:	b₄ = 40


		c.	Show that bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁ for each
			integer k ≥ 2, where a₁, a₂, a₃, ... is the
			sequence defined in exercise 17.

		Suppose k ∈ ℤ and k ≥ 2, and aₙ : A → C, and bₙ : A → B

		We must show that to move k disks from A to B, first 
		we must move the k - 1 smaller disks from A to C, then 
		move the largest disk once from A to B, and finally move 
		the k - 1 smaller disks from C to B.

			bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁

		Before the largest disk can move from A to B, the other
		k - 1 disks must be moved from A to C, which requires
		aₖ₋₁ moves.

		Then the largest disk is free to move once.

		Now the k - 1 smaller disks have to be transferred from
		C to B, and because C is an end pole and B is the middle
		pole, this is the same type of transfer counted by bₙ,
		and thus requires bₖ₋₁ moves.

		And in order for this to make sense, we need at least
		two disks.

		Therefore bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁

Solution:	bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁ for each integer k ≥ 2.


		d.	Show that bₖ ≤ 3bₖ₋₁ + 1 for each integer
			k ≥ 2.

		Suppose k ∈ ℤ and k ≥ 2, and bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁

		We know that aₖ₋₁ is the minimum number of moves
		needed to transfer k - 1 disks from A to C.

		One possible way to transfer k - 1 disks from A to C
		is to first transfer them from A to B, and then from
		B to C.

		Each of these transfers requires bₖ₋₁ moves. Hence:

			aₖ₋₁ ≤ 2bₖ₋₁

		Now we can use this inequality:

			bₖ = aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁ ≤ 2bₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁
			
				2bₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁ = 3bₖ₋₁ + 1

		Therefore:
			
			aₖ₋₁ + 1 + bₖ₋₁ ≤ 3bₖ₋₁ + 1
			bₖ ≤ 3bₖ₋₁ + 1

Solution:	bₖ ≤ 3bₖ₋₁ + 1 for each integer k ≥ 2.
